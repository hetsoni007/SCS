Of the {{threads}} outreach threads I audited, {{engaged.word}} turned into a genuine conversation. The company is one I'll call Quest.

The email that did it had no pitch and no call ask. It asked three specific technical questions about a decision that was visible on their site. They answered in detail, and then they offered the call themselves.

Here it is next to an email of mine that got zero replies. One note first: Quest's email was about Quest's site, so I'm not reprinting it. The version below keeps its exact shape, an opening that names a visible decision followed by three questions and nothing else, applied to a made-up company. The generic email is real and verbatim.

::::compare
:::annotated title="The generic email" verdict="Zero replies" tone="bad" source="Real, verbatim"
> Hope you're doing well!
Filler. It tells them nothing and costs them a line. It's also how every cold email opens, so it marks yours as one before they've read anything else.
> I help founders build apps fast and affordably —
A claim with no proof, and the same one every studio makes. "Fast" and "affordably" are words, not evidence.
> would love to grab 15 minutes if you're open to it.
The whole point of the email, and the part they have the least reason to agree to. It asks for their time before giving them one reason to spend it.
:::
:::annotated title="The Quest-shaped email" verdict="Answered in detail, call offered" tone="good" source="Same structure, made-up company"
> Subject: your PWA-first launch
Names the thing. They know what it's about before they open it.
> Saw you launched as an installable web app instead of going native first.
A visible decision, stated neutrally. It proves you looked, and it doesn't assume it was a mistake.
> Was PWA-first a deliberate call, or a stage-one thing until the product settles?
Question one asks why. It assumes they had a reason, because they usually did.
> How are you handling push notifications on iOS? Relying on people adding it to their home screen, or skipping push for now?
Question two goes where the decision bites. Only someone who knows the platform would ask it, so the competence is shown rather than claimed.
> And is a native app on the roadmap, or does the web app carry you for a while?
Question three asks about their direction, which is the thing people most like to explain.
> Het
No pitch, no calendar link, no "I help founders". The signature is the only thing about me in the email.
:::
::::

## Why the second one works

**It makes no claims, so there's nothing to doubt.** The generic email asks the reader to believe "fast and affordably". The Quest-shaped one asks them to believe nothing. It just shows its working.

**It proves competence instead of asserting it.** Anyone can type "I build apps". Not everyone knows where a PWA-first launch gets awkward. The questions are the credential.

**It asks for something easy to give.** Answering a good question about your own product is easy, and a little flattering. Booking a call with a stranger is neither.

**It leaves the next move to them.** That's why the call came from Quest's side. People who offer a call turn up curious. People who get asked for one turn up guarded, if they turn up at all.

## Writing your own

:::steps
1. **Find a visible decision, not a flaw.** A platform choice, a pricing structure, a checkout flow, a stack. A decision gives you something to ask about. A flaw only gives you something to criticize.
2. **Ask three questions you'd ask a peer.** Why they chose it, where it bites, what's next.
3. **Delete everything about you.** Your services, your clients, your calendar link. If the questions are good, they'll ask who you are.
4. **Only ask what you actually want to know.** Faked curiosity reads as faked. "Genuine" isn't decoration here. It's the part they respond to.
:::

The lesson, in one line: a specific, genuine technical observation is the pitch.
