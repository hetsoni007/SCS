Signal-based targeting / expands §3 of the audit

Search for what people say, not what they’re called

A job title tells you who could buy. A sentence someone posted this week tells you who’s thinking about it right now.

From
Het Soni
Date
27 Sep 2026
Read
4 min

Most prospecting starts with a title filter. Founder or CTO, 11 to 50 people, some industry. Search, export, write.

What you get is everyone who could buy from you. Almost none of them are thinking about your problem this month. You’re interrupting people at random and calling it targeting.

Signal-based targeting flips the order. You search for the words people use when they’re in the middle of the thing you do, and only then look at who said them.

Phrases worth searching

The three that started it for me were “redesigning our site”, “website is live” and “our site is so slow”. Here are more from the same three families. Swap in your own niche: the phrases change, the method doesn’t.

Just launched. The site or app is new and they’re watching it.

  • “website is live”
  • “we just launched”
  • “just went live”
  • “our new site is up”

Rebuild under way. A decision is being made right now.

  • “redesigning our site”
  • “rebuilding our website”
  • “new website coming soon”
  • “migrating our site”

Stated pain. They’ve already diagnosed a problem, in public.

  • “our site is so slow”
  • “site keeps going down”
  • “app keeps crashing”
  • “checkout is broken”

Running the search on LinkedIn

  1. Type the phrase in straight double quotes in the main search bar: "website is live". Quotes force an exact match. LinkedIn only honors straight quotes, so curly ones pasted from a doc won’t work.
  2. Switch the results to Posts.
  3. Sort by Latest, so you see today’s posts rather than the most-liked one from last year.
  4. Set Date posted to Past week, or Past 24 hours for busy phrases.
  5. Combine phrases with an uppercase OR: "website is live" OR "we just launched". LinkedIn only treats AND, OR and NOT as operators when they’re in capitals.

Then, before anything else, open the author’s profile. “We just launched” might come from the founder. It might also come from a social media manager, or from the agency that built the site, which makes them a competitor rather than a prospect.

The same phrases work in Google: site:linkedin.com/posts "website is live", with the date range set under Tools.

Why intent beats titles

Timing. A title stays true for years. A signal is true this week, so it tells you when to write, not just who to write to.

Relevance. You don’t have to guess whether they care about site speed. They told you.

A reason to write. Your first line is a response to something they said, not an interruption.

There’s a quieter fourth reason: signals filter out the wrong audience. Searching for anyone “hiring” drags in recruiters and roles that are already filled. That was the single biggest leak in my own outreach: 50+ of 201 threads went to people who were never going to buy. Nobody posts “our site is so slow” on behalf of a company they don’t work for.

The 0–90 day window

A signal isn’t just a phrase. It’s a phrase with a timestamp, and the timestamp matters as much as the words. Here’s how I think about the life of a launch.

Read it left to right.

Day 0, the launch. The site goes live. The team is watching everything: analytics, feedback, whatever’s breaking. Performance is a live topic, because they’ve just put weeks into this and want it to land.

The first few weeks. The best time to write. A note about their mobile load time lands in the middle of a conversation they’re already having. They may already suspect the thing you found, so your email is a second opinion, not an unwelcome first mention.

Toward day 90. Attention fades as other priorities come back. The site is “done”, even if it isn’t. Your finding still matters, but it’s competing with the rest of the roadmap.

After day 90. Background noise. The launch isn’t news anymore, even inside the company.

That’s why reaching out on day 3 versus day 120 is a different email even with identical copy. The words don’t change. What changes is whether they land on an open question or a closed one.

The window isn’t a law. A company still visibly struggling with performance on day 150 is a live signal. But by then the signal is the struggle, not the launch. Score what’s true today.
